# Kaneko-group cell models with catalytic networks, and how chemca relates (2026-10-24)

For N. Literature check on Kaneko's simulations of cells containing autocatalytic networks, plus
the closest spatial relatives (Hogeweg's group), against the chemca design
(`Research/Chem/plans/2026-10-23_chem-model-v2.md`). PDFs are in `Docs/references/kaneko/` and
`Docs/references/hogeweg/`; bibliographic entries are in `Docs/references/references.md`. I read
the model sections of the key papers and the abstracts of the rest. Where I state a detail, it is
from the paper. Interpretations are marked as mine.

## Summary

- **Yes, chemca is heading toward a 2D-lattice relative of the Kaneko cell models.** It is not a copy, though. The Kaneko line differs from chemca in four ways that matter:
  1. **No genome.** The reaction network is fixed per run, and a cell inherits only its chemical composition (split almost equally at division). Differentiation into cell types is a dynamical instability ("isologous diversification"), not genetic change.
  2. **Evolution, where present, is outside the simulation.** Networks are mutated and selected in a generational loop: n mothers, m mutants each, keep the fastest-dividing (Furusawa & Kaneko 2005; 2000 compares random networks rather than evolving them in place).
  3. **Growth is mass, not energy.** A cell grows by taking up penetrable nutrients and converting them into impenetrable chemicals, and divides when its volume (total content) doubles. There is no free energy and no waste toxicity. Supply comes from a chemical bath at the boundary.
  4. **Space is a continuum medium.** Cells sit in a diffusing medium (a 2D grid in the 1998 *Artificial Life* paper, a 1D chain in the 2000 PRL) and push neighbors aside on division. There is no eviction.
- **chemca would combine both kinds of inheritance in situ**: heritable reaction rates (rgenes), plus compositional inheritance (stores split 50/50), with selection happening on the lattice by eviction rather than in an external loop. As far as I found, that combination (Kaneko-style compositional dynamics, genetic rates and in-situ spatial selection) is not what the Kaneko papers do. That is my reading of the papers I checked, not an exhaustive search.
- **Two results transfer directly as hypotheses for chemca:**
  - Isologous diversification: genetically identical cells can split into distinct compositional types through intracellular instability plus coupling through the medium. chemca could show this with `mu_rgene = 0`, and it needs a readout (clustering cells by store composition and dynamics, independent of genes).
  - Minority-molecule control (Kaneko & Yomo 2002; Kamimura & Kaneko 2010): a slowly replicated, scarce species becomes the carrier of heredity and controls the cell's state. In gs3, the activator A, inherited at division and needed for metabolism, is a candidate minority-control molecule.
- **The rate law matches.** Kaneko's 1998/2000 cells use catalysis quadratic in the catalyst, `c_substrate · c_catalyst^α` with α = 2 ("in most simulations"), which is the gs3 order-2 gene (F + 2A → 3A). Takagi & Kaneko (2001) extend Gray-Scott itself to many chemicals and find spots that replicate and differentiate. That is the RD analog of the evolutionary Gray-Scott direction.
- **Hogeweg's group is the closest spatial and evolutionary relative, and it has spirals.** Boerlijst & Hogeweg (1991) showed that hypercycles on a lattice (a CA) self-organize into **spiral waves**, which make them resistant to parasites: selection acts at the level of spirals. Takeuchi & Hogeweg (2009) compare spatial self-organization (traveling waves) with explicit compartments as two routes to multilevel selection. Given N's interest in spirals and our [CHEM-N6](../../Research/Chem/ISSUES.md)/N7 wave results, this line probably matters more for chemca's evolutionary questions than the Kaneko cell papers do.

## What this means for chemca (my reading)

1. **Reproduction gate: energy (chemca's default) or mass (Kaneko)?**
   - Kaneko's growth-by-uptake-and-division-at-doubling is §4's option (iii), biomass, which I had deferred.
   - The Kaneko results show it alone suffices for rich behavior (differentiation, Zipf, life cycles), with no thermodynamics.
   - chemca's energy currency adds what Kaneko lacks: waste, toxicity, and a free-energy budget, which is where our waves come from.
   - Worth offering both as a gate option: `repro_gate = 'energy' | 'mass'`. With mass, impenetrable compounds (transport u = 0) are the body, and division happens at doubling.
   - This is a design question for N, not something to build silently. The engine agent is building the energy gate.
2. **Compositional heredity is already in chemca**, because stores split 50/50. So chemca can test isologous diversification in situ by switching genes off (`mu_rgene = 0`, identical genes) and asking whether cells split into compositional types. That needs a readout: clustering of per-cell store vectors and their time series. It is cheap to add to `run_chemca`.
3. **Network size.** Kaneko's interesting cases need k of about 20 species and several paths each, with only about 5% of random networks giving exponential growth. chemca's stage 3 (`n_chem > 2`, (A, B, C, k) genes) is where this lives. In chemca the network is per-cell and heritable, so it can evolve in situ rather than in an external loop. That is a genuine difference and a candidate research question: does in-situ spatial selection find Kaneko's "cooperative, differentiating" networks?
4. **Spirals and multilevel selection** (Boerlijst & Hogeweg). Our food/waste waves ([CHEM-N6](../../Research/Chem/ISSUES.md)) and the [CHEM-N7](../../Research/Chem/ISSUES.md) result (genotype lifetimes collapse in the wave regime, yet the egene count rises) look like the beginning of selection mediated by spatial pattern. The hypercycle literature says spirals can turn selection into selection among spirals. That is a concrete thing to look for in vanilla food/waste (Track A) as well as in chemca.
5. **Rate law and toxicity.** The α = 2 catalysis is consistent with gs3. Kaneko's models have no toxicity, so "need some, die of too much" (§6) is our addition. In Kaneko's cells the analogous tension is the critical nutrient uptake D_c: too much uptake leaves no room for catalysts, too little starves.

### Synthesis

**Signatures of pattern-level selection, as used in the papers (Paper):**

1. Lineage tracing at the pattern core. Savill et al. 1997 mark all individuals in a spiral core at t=0 and follow descendants; most of the spiral, and eventually much of the turbulence, descends from the core. Boerlijst & Hogeweg 1991 make the same point about spirals.
2. Position-resolved trait or lineage profiles. Colizzi & Hogeweg 2016 and von der Dunk et al. 2017 separate selection at the front from selection at the back of a wave; von der Dunk et al. plot composition against rank in the wave front.
3. Invasion and competition assays between pattern types. Boerlijst et al. 1993 (via Savill 1997) and Savill & Hogeweg 1999 place two types side by side and record which pattern or type excludes the other; Colizzi & Hogeweg 2016 do the same with different k_a.
4. A predictive spatial index. Colizzi & Hogeweg 2016 show that the amount of uninterrupted empty space in front of fronts predicts the evolutionary regime better than population-level statistics. Savill et al. 1997 add that population-level statistics can give the wrong answer.
5. Wave life-history events: wave birth (escape from the back), annihilation by collision, splitting, aging within a wave, wave count and size over evolutionary time (Takeuchi & Hogeweg 2007, 2009; Hogeweg & Takeuchi 2003).
6. Controlled perturbation: periodic ablation of patches of a defined size (Colizzi & Hogeweg 2016), and contrasts with well-mixed or high-diffusion controls.
7. What is selected at the wave level: fecundity (new waves), not longevity, when the pattern is long-lived (Takeuchi & Hogeweg 2009). Faster dispersal wins in wave regimes unless dispersal is costly (Savill & Hogeweg 1999).

**Suggestions for EvoCA (not from the papers):**

- S1. Core-lineage marking. In the [CHEM-N7](../../Research/Chem/ISSUES.md) wave regime, tag all alive cells within a radius of an identified wave source at t0 (using the source detection of the N6 machinery) and follow descendants by lineage id. Report the fraction of the population descended from source-region cells, against the same quantity for random regions of equal size and equal initial population. This tests directly whether wave sources are where lineage persistence is concentrated, and would explain why activity-above-drift collapses (lineages are carried through the medium rather than staying put) while egene count still rises.
- S2. Invasion assay of a waste trait into wave vs non-wave regimes. Once wgenes exist, fix a resident population, seed a small cluster of high-excretion (cheater-like or sociopathic) or low-excretion types, and record fixation, loss, and time to loss, in the wave-regime parameter set and in a matched parameter set without waves (for instance lower waste toxicity, as in the one-way/two-way cases), with a well-mixed (high gdiff or shuffled) control. Following Colizzi & Hogeweg, add periodic ablations of defined size to see whether the opening of empty space changes the sign of selection on the trait.
- S3. Front/back position profiles. For each wgene or egene value, compute its mean and the lineage age as a function of distance behind the nearest wave front (rank in front R, as in von der Dunk et al.). The prediction from the papers is differentiation between front and back; in EvoCA the front is where food is high and waste is low.
- S4. Wave-level fitness. Count wave births, annihilations, and splits (the Takeuchi events) as a function of the mean wgene value in the wave, to test whether waves with different excretion rates differ in fecundity (new sources) rather than longevity.
- S5. Spatial regime index. Define an EvoCA analogue of "uninterrupted empty space ahead of the front" (for instance, the distance a front travels through unoccupied or high-food territory before the next collision) and check whether it predicts the direction of selection on eating genes, as the index does for k_a.
- S6. Pattern competition. Initialize two half-planes with resident genotypes that differ in a trait that changes the wave period (e.g., egene count, later wgenes); the faster wave should win by annihilating the slower, per Boerlijst et al. 1993. Cheap to run with `run_sim` and the existing site_period statistic.
- S7. Caution. In this literature the wave structure is imposed by parasite-host dynamics with local replication; EvoCA's waves are excitation waves in a food/waste medium, and every cell carries both a genotype and a metabolic role. The papers' wave-level selection is on replicators that are reborn in each wave front; whether EvoCA cells (which persist and reproduce into neighbors) show the same front/back splitting is an open question, and S1 and S3 are the way to find out. The shared-statistics warning from Savill et al. 1997 applies directly to the activity-above-drift measure: it is an aggregate and may mislead about what the pattern level is doing.

Five-line synthesis for the hand-off:

1. Wave-level selection in this literature acts on wave fecundity (new waves), not longevity; the waves age, and new waves are born from the front side.
2. The measurable signatures are core/source lineage marking, front-versus-back trait profiles, side-by-side invasion assays, and a spatial index such as empty space ahead of fronts.
3. Population-level aggregate statistics can mislead (Savill et al. 1997), which may explain EvoCA's collapsed activity-above-drift despite a directional trait.
4. A social waste trait maps onto the replicator/parasite and catalyst/template conflicts; ablation and diffusion contrasts show when space rescues the cooperative type.
5. The first EvoCA measurements to try are S1 (source-core lineage tracing) and S2 (wgene invasion in wave vs non-wave regimes).
